## Introduction: A Ubiquitous Workplace Moment

Artificial intelligence hands you a seemingly complete business plan in seconds. Without giving it a second thought, you take it at face value.

Months later, the team asks you to exercise independent judgment on a similar matter—and you find you cannot explain, for the life of you, the reasoning behind the choices.

This is not a simple dispute over "whether to use the tool." It is a deeper **question of where cognitive assets are located**. Over the past several years, around this question, cognitive scientists and frontier practitioners around the world have formed two sharply opposed, yet forcefully argued, positions. Both sides hold firsthand experience and a solid base of empirical data. The aim of this essay is to peel back the layers and place the strongest arguments of the two routes side by side, then to clarify the underlying mechanism that draws the line between them.

---

## The First Route: Judgment Must Be Internalized Into the Brain

**Proponent: Barbara Oakley**

Oakley has long worked in the mechanics of cognition and in engineering education, and is the author of *A Mind for Numbers*. In a special symposium at the 2026 International Congress on Mathematical Education (ICME), she made a pointed observation:

> "AI shows you the answer, but the one who actually has to understand the matter is you."

Oakley's argument rests not on moral exhortation but on the neurological workings of the human brain. The human brain contains two memory circuits, each with its own job: **declarative memory**, governed by the hippocampus (slow to retrieve, effortful, and capable of being recited clearly), and **procedural memory**, governed by the striatum (running on autopilot, fast to respond, and sunk deep into intuition). For a skill to truly settle in, it must pass through the full neurodevelopmental cycle of "deliberate practice, overlearning, and compressive refinement."

There is no shortcut past this physiological compression. If the algorithm does the whole job on your behalf, the learner's brain bypasses the necessary refinement circuitry and falls into the trap of **skill suspension (the "never-skilling" state)**: cognition stays stuck at the surface layer of "seeming to understand it" and cannot cross over into the deep water of "doing it independently."

The scholars who back this route form a solid chain of cognitive-science evidence:

- **Nelson Cowan (2001)**: revised Miller's famous "7±2" hypothesis; through rigorous experiments on attentional breadth, he established that the hard bandwidth of human central working memory is only about **4 chunks** and cannot be expanded through training after the fact. The physiological channel through which humans process complex information is inherently very narrow.
- **Fernand Gobet**: his CHREST cognitive architecture shows that the "intuition" of an expert is no mystical flash of insight, but the automatic activation of highly organized structures (chunks and lookahead search trees) residing in long-term memory. **The expert's edge lies in the density of the long-term memory store, not in raw computation in the moment.**
- **K. Anders Ericsson**: his 25-year empirical review of expert performance finds that the essence of superior performance is precisely the long-term network of chunks deposited through massive deliberate practice.

From this, the first route's central claim follows: **the more powerful the tool, the more fragile the untrained individual becomes.** The smooth output of an algorithm readily induces the cognitive illusion of "I've already got this"; and the only remedy for a brain with limited bandwidth is to steadily compress core judgment into long-term memory, not to throw it outward.

## The Second Route: Cognition Must Be Externalized into Governed Knowledge Assets

**Proponents: Garry Tan and Andrej Karpathy**

Garry Tan, speaking about "personal AGI" at a YC incubation camp, put it this way:

> "Either you build skill files that are your own, or your job will become someone else's skill file."

This route holds that in the age of large language models, the strategic leverage has shifted from the model's underlying weights to the context that the writer supplies—namely the individual's deposited prompt strategies, business workflows, decision criteria, and canonical cases. Only by formalizing this thinking into version-controlled plain-text files and revising them continuously can one achieve compounding returns on one's cognitive assets. If one does not actively record and take ownership, the individual is, in effect, merely renting external compute power.

This line of thought can be traced back to Paul Graham's philosophy of writing: clarifying muddled thinking through clear writing, where the very act of letting text settle is itself a process of cognitive growth.

Computer scientist Karpathy reinforced this route from the level of system architecture. He described the evolutionary paradigm of software engineering from 1.0 (humans hand-writing the rules in code), to 2.0 (optimizing weights driven by data), to 3.0 (guiding model generation in natural language). In this context, the professional's core duty becomes the architectural design of "context engineering." Faced with the model's irregular, jagged capability boundary, the human's role is to act as the rigorous commander.

The second route's central claim thereby takes shape: **the more powerful the tool, the more effective an externalized knowledge asset becomes.** Since the human working bandwidth is only about 4 items, scarce physiological bandwidth should not be spent on rote memorization and mechanical retrieval. Outsourcing cognition into executable, shareable, iterable document assets—while the human concentrates on being the judge and the director—fits the engineering logic and builds a compounding advantage that outlasts bodily memory.

## The True Point of Divergence: Who Bears the Final Say After Externalization

Viewed from a purely theoretical standpoint, the two routes are not irreconcilable at the level of evidence. Oakley stresses that because the brain's bandwidth is narrow, it must be compressed to the essentials; Garry Tan argues that precisely because the brain's bandwidth is narrow, the complex rules should be handed off to external text. The two are each the other's premise.

**The real divergence comes in exactly one place: where the judgment ultimately lands, and the mechanism for testing the cognitive conclusions.**

- What Oakley worries about is that blind externalization collapses the cognitive frame of reference. Without an internalized base of knowledge, the writer will be powerless to distinguish truth from fabrication in what the algorithm produces;
- What Garry Tan emphasizes is that structured externalization yields compounding returns on assets—on the condition that these texts belong to the individual and sit under rigorous, continuous correction.

This shows the dispute is not "whether you should record or use tools," but rather: **once thinking is handed off to an external bearer, who is responsible for the final review and for bearing the consequences?**

Around this crux, the scholars added finer-grained dimensions:

Ethan Mollick proposed a **rule of three for tasks**:
- **Fully internalized tasks (Just Me)**: the value judgments and ethical calls that only a human can carry;
- **Co-delegated tasks (Delegated)**: the algorithm drafts, but a human checks it throughout against internalized standards;
- **Fully automated tasks (Automated)**: well-bounded, routine steps that a machine can run end to end.

Gary Marcus and Yann LeCun sounded the alarm from the foundational-technology side. Marcus has persistently argued for the necessity of coupling symbolic systems with neural ones, warning of the hallucination risk of purely probabilistic generation; LeCun insists on the path of world models, pointing out plainly that autoregressive large language models carry a physical defect in commonsense reasoning.

Tracing the history of ideas, the Japanese scholar Hidehiko Nogiri, in Chapter 2 of *The Age of the Brain*, published in 1985, had long since noted that the gradual outsourcing of human cognitive functions to external media—from oracle bones, wood, and silk, to paper and code—is an inevitable structure of civilizational evolution. The question he left behind is still live: **the point was never whether to offload cognition, but where the offloaded knowledge assets actually reside, and who governs them.**

## The Inverting Variable: The Verifiability of the Domain

Bringing these theoretical threads together, there is only one key variable that resolves the opposition between the two routes: **whether the matter at hand has an objective, low-cost verifier.**

Look at two representative paradigms from recent years:

- **Text-form skill descriptions**: they do externalize experience into a readable workflow, but if the source judgment is biased, not only can nothing auto-correct it—it amplifies the bias. This is a **weak-check environment**;
- **Codenizing mathematical proofs (Proof-as-code)**: for example, the interactive theorem-proving in Lean that mathematician Alex Kontorovich advocates, in which the syntax rules of the formal system act as an impartial judge—any gap in the logic fails to compile. This is a **strong-check environment**.

Taking **verifiability** as the horizontal axis, the split in knowledge practice becomes at a glance clear:

| Domain of practice | Strength of verifier | Judging mechanism | Best strategy |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| **Formal mathematics, software testing, tasks with definite ground truth** | **Strong** | Compiler and automated tests directly judge right from wrong | **Actively externalize**, and fully exploit compute |
| **Drafting writing, drafting plans, analysis with evaluation criteria** | **Medium** | Depends on expert human review; the cost of rechecking is high | **Externalize cautiously and retain rigorous human review** |
| **Value orientations, making meaning, bearing final responsibility** | **None** | No objective check algorithm; it is all decided by the subject | **Resolutely internalize into the brain**, no cheap outsourcing allowed |

From this, the essence of the intellectual contest becomes clear: it is not "choosing a side between the internalizers and the externalizers," but "drawing the boundary between strong-check and weak-check domains." In domains with ironclad verifiers, algorithmic automation can unleash productivity enormously; but in domains lacking an objective yardstick, if you abandon internalized aesthetic, ethical, and logical intuition, the only thing externalization compounds is mediocrity and error.

## The Full Lineage: Cognition's Evolution in Four Acts

```
1985                 2001                 2006                 2012                 2017                 2024                 2026
│                    │                    │                    │                    │                    │                    │
Act I                Act I                Act II               Act II               Act III              Act III              Act IV
Mechanism            Mechanism            Expert               Expert               Model rise           Model reflect        Asset & verify
Nogiri               Cowan                Ericsson             Gobet                Transformer          Marcus               Tan, Karpathy
Age of Brain         4-item limit         Deliberate practice  Chunk networks       Generative route     Diminishing returns  Theorem-as-code
```

1. **Stage One (1985–2001): Founding the Physiological Mechanisms**
   Nogiri established the evolutionary framework for the externalization of cognition; Miller's "7±2" hypothesis was empirically sharpened by Cowan to "central working memory holds only about 4 items," locking in the upper bound of human physiological bandwidth.
2. **Stage Two (2006–2012): The Maturation of the Expert-Chunk Theories**
   Ericsson's empirical review confirmed the theory of deliberate practice; Gobet, through computational modeling, showed that the hierarchical chunks in long-term memory are the source of intuition, securing the irreplaceable status of the "internalized knowledge base."
3. **Stage Three (2017–2024): The Surge of Large Models and the Reflection on Their Boundaries**
   Generative AI swept the world; Marcus and LeCun critiqued the defects of pure probabilistic generation from the differentiable and the symbolic ends; Mollick exposed the jagged character of tool capabilities.
4. **Stage Four (2025 to present): Convergence on Assets and Verifiability**
   Garry Tan proposed the assetization of skill files; Karpathy advanced Software 3.0; Oakley raised the alarm over learning degradation; formalized code proofs set a hard acceptance gate for externalized thinking. The center of gravity of human thought has converged, from "can the algorithm replace the human," entirely to "where core cognition should live, and who holds the final review."

## Three Self-Checks for the Writer

You do not need to memorize abstract jargon from the theory. Before each time you turn on AI for writing or analysis, run through these three questions in order:

1. **Is there an objective verifier for the current task?**
   If the code can pass a unit test, or the conclusion can be falsified by a logical theorem, feel free to lean hard on the tool and explore;
2. **If there is no ready-made verifier, who is responsible for checking and gating?**
   If you are the one bearing final review, then the aesthetic standards and common-sense facts you need to judge the quality of the draft must be established in advance, independent of the tool;
3. **Who ultimately owns the thinking assets that have settled down?**
   If the output merely scatters through a platform's ephemeral conversation stream, the knowledge has not truly been retained. Only text that has been read by a human, refined from the chaff, and brought into a controlled version repository belongs to the writer's own cognitive assets.
